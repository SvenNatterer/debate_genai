# Philosopher's Guide

A short introduction to every thinker in the library — who they were and why they still matter.

---

## The Ancient Greeks

### Socrates (470–399 BCE)
Socrates never wrote anything down. Instead he walked through Athens asking people questions — about justice, courage, what it means to live well — until they realised they did not know what they thought they knew. This method of finding truth through dialogue is the starting point of Western philosophy. He was sentenced to death for supposedly corrupting the young, and chose to drink poison rather than escape. His most famous line: *"I know that I know nothing."*

### Plato (428–348 BCE)
A student of Socrates and teacher of Aristotle. Plato believed that the physical world around us is just a pale copy of a higher reality made of perfect, timeless **Forms** — the Form of Beauty, Justice, the Good. His *Republic* describes an ideal state governed by philosopher-kings. He founded the Academy in Athens, one of the earliest universities in history.

### Aristotle (384–322 BCE)
Where Plato looked upward to abstract ideals, Aristotle looked carefully at the actual world. He studied and classified everything — animals, governments, drama, arguments — and argued that the goal of human life is **eudaimonia** (roughly: flourishing), reached through virtue and practical wisdom. He was tutor to Alexander the Great and wrote foundational works in logic, biology, ethics and politics.

### Hypatia of Alexandria (~360–415 CE)
The most famous female philosopher of the ancient world. Hypatia was a mathematician and Neoplatonic philosopher who led the great school of Alexandria and attracted students from across the Mediterranean. She taught that careful reason and mathematics could reveal the deeper order of the cosmos. She was killed by a Christian mob in 415 CE — an event that marks the end of classical pagan philosophy in the ancient world.

## The Medieval World

### Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274)
A Dominican friar and the greatest intellect of medieval Christian philosophy. Aquinas argued that **faith and reason are not enemies** — God gave humans reason precisely so they could understand the world and, through it, God. He took Aristotle's philosophy and built it into the foundations of Catholic theology. His five arguments for the existence of God remain famous to this day.

## The Early Modern Period

### René Descartes (1596–1650)
Often called the father of modern philosophy. Descartes decided to doubt absolutely everything that could possibly be doubted, looking for one thing he could be truly certain of. He found it: *"I think, therefore I am."* The very act of doubting proves that the doubter exists. From this one solid point he tried to rebuild all human knowledge. He also gave us the famous **mind–body problem**: if the mind is non-physical, how does it interact with a physical body?

### John Locke (1632–1704)
Locke argued that we are born with no knowledge at all — the mind starts as a **blank slate**, and everything we know comes from experience. In politics he held that governments only have authority because people give it to them, and exist to protect natural rights: life, liberty, and property. These ideas fed directly into the American Declaration of Independence.

### Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716)
A mathematician of genius — he and Newton invented calculus independently of each other. Leibniz also built one of the most ambitious philosophical systems in history, arguing that reality is made of infinite mind-like units called **monads**, each one reflecting the whole universe from its own point of view. He claimed we live in **the best of all possible worlds**, a view Voltaire famously mocked in the novel *Candide*.

## The Enlightenment & 19th Century

### Mary Wollstonecraft (1759–1797)
The founding figure of Western feminism. In *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792), Wollstonecraft argued that women are not naturally less capable than men — they only appear so because they are denied education and treated as decorations rather than rational human beings. Reason and virtue belong to all people equally, regardless of sex.

### Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)
Perhaps the most important philosopher since Aristotle. Kant argued that our minds do not just passively receive reality — they actively shape it, imposing categories like space, time, and cause. In ethics he rejected consequences entirely: what makes an action right is whether it follows **duty**, tested by his famous *categorical imperative* — act only according to rules you could want everyone to follow. His work reshaped every area of philosophy.

### Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel (1770–1831)
Hegel's philosophy is notoriously hard to read, but enormously influential. He argued that ideas and history move forward through **contradiction**: one idea (thesis) meets its opposite (antithesis) and they combine into something new (synthesis). He saw all of history as **Spirit** (Geist) — a kind of collective human consciousness — gradually waking up to itself. Marx borrowed this structure and turned it in a completely different direction.

### John Stuart Mill (1806–1873)
The great defender of **utilitarianism**: an action is right if it produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number of people. Mill added that not all pleasures are equal — the satisfaction of reading or thinking is worth more than simple physical pleasure. In *On Liberty* he argued that the only reason society can ever restrict a person's freedom is to prevent harm to others. He also supported women's suffrage long before it was mainstream.

### Karl Marx (1818–1883)
Marx argued that history is driven by **class struggle** — the conflict between those who own factories and resources and those who must work for them. He believed capitalism **alienates** workers, turning human labour into just another commodity. He predicted capitalism would eventually collapse and be replaced by a classless society. Whether or not this prediction came true, his analysis of power, economic inequality and ideology changed politics and social science permanently.

## The 20th Century

### Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900)
Nietzsche declared that **"God is dead"** — meaning that modern science and reason had destroyed the foundations on which traditional morality rested, leaving a dangerous void. He criticised what he called **slave morality** — values like meekness and self-sacrifice, which he saw as the resentment of the weak disguised as virtue. He called instead for **self-overcoming** and the courage to create new values. He was badly misused by the Nazis; his actual philosophy has nothing to do with nationalism or racism.

### Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889–1951)
Wittgenstein is one of those rare thinkers who produced two completely different and equally influential philosophies. His early work argued that language maps the logical structure of reality. His later work reversed this: **meaning is not about mapping reality but about use** — words mean what they do within the shared practices and contexts of human life. Most philosophical problems, he thought, are simply what happens when language is used outside the situations that give it meaning.

### Hannah Arendt (1906–1975)
One of the most original political thinkers of the 20th century, shaped by her own experience as a Jewish refugee from Nazi Germany. In *The Origins of Totalitarianism* she traced how both Nazism and Stalinism came to be. Her coverage of the trial of Nazi official Adolf Eichmann gave us the phrase **"the banality of evil"** — the idea that great crimes are often carried out by ordinary, thoughtless people, not monsters. She believed active political participation was one of the highest things humans could do.

### Jean-Paul Sartre (1906–1980)
The central voice of **existentialism**: his key claim is that *"existence precedes essence"* — there is no God to define human nature in advance, so we simply exist first and then must **choose** who we are through our actions. This makes humans radically free, but also completely responsible for themselves. Sartre called this situation being **"condemned to be free."** He refused the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1964, saying a writer who accepts institutional honours loses their independence.

### Simone de Beauvoir (1908–1986)
Existentialist philosopher, novelist, and one of the most important feminist thinkers of the 20th century. Her most famous line is: *"One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman."* Femininity, she argued, is not a biological fact but something that society constructs and imposes. She applied existentialist ideas about freedom and responsibility to the real, concrete situation of women — asking what authentic freedom looks like when structural inequality shapes your choices from birth.

### Philippa Foot (1920–2010)
Foot revived **virtue ethics** in analytic philosophy at a time when most philosophers had moved on to rule-based or consequentialist theories. She argued that morality is rooted in human nature — what is good for humans is tied to what allows them to live and function well as human beings. She is also the inventor of the **trolley problem** (1967): a runaway trolley is heading for five people — do you pull a lever to divert it, killing one person instead? It is now the most discussed thought experiment in ethics.

### Judith Butler (1956–)
Butler's *Gender Trouble* (1990) is one of the most cited academic books of the last fifty years. She argued that **gender is performative** — not the expression of a hidden inner identity, but something produced by the repeated acts, gestures and behaviours that social norms demand of us. This does not mean gender is a free choice or a costume; the pressure to perform it correctly is very real and the consequences of failing to do so can be severe. Her work is foundational to gender studies and queer theory.

## A Reading Order

If you are new to philosophy, a natural path through these thinkers:

1. **Socrates → Plato → Aristotle** — the founding conversation
2. **Aquinas** — how ancient thought survived into the medieval world
3. **Descartes → Locke → Leibniz → Kant** — the rise of modern philosophy
4. **Hegel → Marx → Nietzsche** — the great 19th-century upheaval
5. **Wittgenstein → Sartre → de Beauvoir → Arendt** — the 20th century
6. **Wollstonecraft → Foot → Butler** — women's voices across the full timeline
